Technical write-up

Replacing FortiGate SSL VPN with a self-hosted NetBird overlay

A three-phase design covering remote access, the recurring operational tasks your team runs today, and the site-to-site tunnels.

Prepared for Justin, Summit Infrastructure Author Flamarion Jorge Status Draft for review

01Summary

Fortinet is removing SSL VPN from FortiOS, and the vendor path means new licences and new infrastructure. You have already proven the alternative works: a NetBird management server on a Linux VM, a routing peer, and a test client reaching 10.120.x.x. This document is about what that becomes in production for 40 people inside a CMMC Level 2 scope.

The design replaces remote access first, then removes recurring manual work, then retires the site-to-site IPsec mesh. Every phase runs in parallel with the system it replaces, so there is no cutover and no moment where remote access depends on something unproven. Three outcomes matter:

  • Access becomes identity-driven. An AD group maps to a NetBird group, which maps to a policy, which grants a specific resource. One source of truth instead of directory and firewall policy drifting apart.
  • Deployment is additive. Phase 1 changes no existing FortiGate rule, route, or tunnel. The overlay is installed beside the firewall, not through it.
  • Auditability improves. Per-user records of who connected, when, and what they reached, tied to identity rather than to a pool address.

02What changes from your proof of concept

You have seen the moving parts already. Three things change on the way to production.

ComponentRoleWhere it sits
Management, Signal, Relay, DashboardAuthentication, policy distribution, peer registry, connection setup, fallback data pathnb-mgmt-01, DMZ
Routing peersBridge the overlay onto existing subnetsnb-rp-01/02, Server VLAN
NetBird clientReplaces FortiClient on user devices40 endpoints

First, the management stack and the routing peer separate onto different hosts in different zones, for the reasons in section 3.2. Second, the datastore moves from the embedded file-based store to PostgreSQL, so state can be backed up live and a high-availability control plane stays possible later. Third, identity stops being local and starts coming from your Active Directory.

The path a remote user takes is worth stating once, because everything else follows from it. The client authenticates to nb-mgmt through your identity provider and receives the policy that says what it may reach. It then opens an encrypted WireGuard tunnel to nb-rp, which forwards the traffic onto the Server VLAN. The destination host sees an ordinary connection from a local address.

03Phase 1 | Replacing SSL VPN for remote users

Move 40 remote users onto the overlay with AD and Duo behind them, while your FortiGate policy set and your site-to-site tunnels stay exactly as they are.

ArchitectureHow the management server and routing peers are built at HQ

Two hosts rather than one. nb-mgmt-01 runs the management API, Signal, Relay, the dashboard and the PostgreSQL datastore. For 40 users a modest VM is enough, on the order of 4 vCPU, 8 GB RAM and 100 GB of disk. nb-rp-01 and nb-rp-02 are the routing peers, deployed as a pair so that high-availability routes can fail over between them.

One sizing note specific to your environment. NetBird prefers direct peer-to-peer tunnels and falls back to relaying when NAT traversal fails. Because you are self-hosting, relayed traffic crosses your own DMZ host and your own internet link rather than a vendor's global infrastructure. At 40 users this is very unlikely to be a capacity problem, but it is a real difference from the hosted model, so the pilot should measure how much traffic actually relays rather than assume.

PlacementWhy the management server belongs in the DMZ and the routing peer does not

The management stack terminates inbound connections from the public internet: every client reaches it to authenticate, to exchange peer state through Signal, and to relay when a direct tunnel cannot be built. That makes it a publicly accessible component, and NIST 800-171 3.13.5 asks for exactly that to sit in a separated subnetwork. Putting it in the Server VLAN behind a NAT would work technically and would be awkward to defend in your SSP.

The routing peer is the opposite. It accepts no inbound connections at all and only ever dials outbound to the management host. It holds a forwarding path into your internal networks, so it belongs inside the trust boundary where your existing controls already apply to it.

The property worth showing your assessor

Because the routing peer always initiates, the DMZ-to-internal rule can be written one way: the Server VLAN may reach the DMZ, the DMZ may not initiate into the Server VLAN. Even if nb-mgmt were fully compromised, it has no path to open a connection inward. Separately, control 3.1.14 asks that remote access be routed through managed access control points, and a single hardened ingress that all 40 users authenticate through is a cleaner answer to that than today's arrangement, where the FortiGate is simultaneously perimeter firewall, VPN concentrator and access-control enforcement.

ReachabilityHow 40 users reach internal resources with FortiGate policies unchanged

Access is modelled with NetBird's Networks construct rather than flat routes. A network contains resources, which are individual hosts, subnets or domains, reachable through designated routing peers, and access is granted by policies binding AD groups to those resources. Nothing is reachable until a policy says so.

For Phase 1 that means one HQ-Datacenter network with resources defined per service, the file server, the ERP application, the internal wiki, the RDP jump hosts, rather than publishing 10.120.0.0/16 as a single resource. Both work; only one of them survives the question "who can reach the ERP server?"

The reason nothing on the FortiGate has to change is that routing peers masquerade by default. Traffic arrives at the destination sourced from the routing peer's own Server VLAN address, so there are no return routes to add, no new subnets in your routing table, and no existing rule to modify. That is what makes a genuinely parallel deployment possible.

The trade-off is per-user attribution at the firewall. All 40 users appear in FortiGate logs as one source IP, where today a session can be tied to a VPN pool address. Since 800-171 3.3.2 requires actions be traceable to individuals, that requirement moves to NetBird's own traffic events rather than disappearing. Section 6 covers why I would resolve it that way rather than by disabling masquerade.

Network changesFirewall, routing and DNS

Everything below is additive. No existing policy is modified.

DirectionWhat is needed
InboundPublic DNS A record and a FortiGate VIP for the management endpoint; TCP 443 and UDP 3478, plus TCP 80 if certificates are issued through ACME
Outboundnb-mgmt on 80/443 for certificate issuance and container images; endpoints allowed outbound UDP where egress filtering applies

One item to get right: exempt the management domain from SSL inspection. Inspecting it breaks certificate pinning and pushes clients onto the relay unnecessarily.

DNS deserves its own note, because the impact is smaller than expected in one direction and larger in another. Nothing changes on the DNS servers themselves: no new zones, no forwarders, no delegation. NetBird is not in the resolution path as a server. It distributes a nameserver configuration to clients, and the client's local resolver decides where each query goes. Scoped to your internal zone by match domain, only internal queries reach 10.120.1.10 and .11, and personal traffic keeps resolving normally, which cuts both support burden and stray query volume.

What does change: the resolvers must exist as resources with a policy permitting UDP 53 through a routing peer, and because of masquerade those queries arrive from the routing peer's address. If your resolvers restrict recursion by source subnet, or run split-horizon views keyed on source, that needs accounting for, and per-user DNS logging is lost the same way firewall attribution is.

Active Directory specifics
  • Domain-joined clients always need a nameserver entry pointing at a domain controller, regardless of other configuration, because a domain resource resolves A and AAAA records only and AD authentication depends on SRV and DC-locator records.
  • NetBird should not manage DNS on the domain controllers themselves. If a DC ever becomes a peer, disable DNS management for its group.
  • On Windows domain controllers the WireGuard port can collide with the DNS Server service, which needs a dnscmd exclusion.

IdentityHow AD and Duo fit, and what improves

NetBird authenticates users over OIDC. Active Directory speaks LDAP and Kerberos, and the Duo Authentication Proxy is a RADIUS and LDAP shim, so neither is an OIDC provider and a federation layer is required. This is the critical path for Phase 1 and it is net-new infrastructure, introduced in parallel with everything else.

OptionHostingNotes
ADFSOn-premisesShortest path where Windows Server licensing exists; mature Duo plugin. No native SCIM, which matters for Phase 2
KeycloakOn-premisesNo Windows dependency; SCIM available through extensions; more design work for Duo
Duo SSODuo-hostedSimplest by far, and Duo is already a cloud dependency in your stack today

What improves is worth being concrete about. Today an AD group grants a user a tunnel, and then FortiGate policies decide what they can reach: two systems, maintained separately, drifting apart over time. In the new model group membership drives the policy that grants the resource, so the directory is the source of truth and offboarding propagates from it. MFA moves from tunnel establishment to identity-provider login, which is where the rest of your access will eventually be enforced too.

Two behaviours to configure deliberately rather than inherit. An OIDC token does not expire the moment an AD account is disabled, so peer login expiration is what bounds that window and should be set as a conscious decision about session lifetime under 3.1.11. And peers enrolled with setup keys, meaning your routing peers and later your CI runners, are machine identities that do not participate in user login expiration, so they need their own key lifecycle.

Policy transitionMoving enforcement from FortiGate to NetBird

Not as a cutover. The two systems run in parallel and the migration goes in four stages, described in section 7. The important sequencing detail is in stage two: before tightening anything, replicate current effective access in NetBird policies. That stage deliberately does not improve your security posture. It establishes equivalence, so that when something breaks during tightening you know it was the tightening and not the migration.

AuditWhat the trail looks like, and how it answers the questionnaire

NetBird produces two distinct kinds of record and your questionnaire needs both. Audit events cover the control plane: who created a policy, who added a peer, who changed a group, who logged in. That is your administrative accountability evidence and it ships in the open-source build. Traffic events cover the data plane: which user, from which peer, reached which resource, when, and whether it was allowed or denied. That is what answers "per-user audit trails showing who connected, when, and what they accessed", and it is a NetBird Enterprise feature.

One item I would confirm rather than assert: your questionnaire likely specifies a retention period and may require export to a SIEM. I want to verify the retention behaviour of the self-hosted event store and the available streaming integrations before committing to an answer there.

Assumptions
  • HQ has a spare public IP or a VIP available on the FortiGate pair, and the DMZ can host new Linux VMs.
  • AD group structure is usable as an authorization source. If groups are organised around file shares rather than access tiers, some structural work comes first.
  • No internal application authorizes or licenses based on client source IP. Worth actively checking, since legacy applications sometimes do.
To confirm

  • Whether the self-hosting requirement extends to the identity provider, and whether you are on Duo Federal.
  • The session lifetime your compliance posture requires, which sets peer login expiration.
  • Whether SSL inspection currently applies to endpoint and DMZ egress, and whether certificates come from ACME or an internal CA.

04Phase 2 | Reducing operational toil

Four recurring tasks. Three of them fall out of the same identity-and-policy model Phase 1 already introduces. One does not, and I would rather say so than stretch.

Removing people who have left In scope

Two mechanisms combine. Because access is granted through group membership, disabling an account in AD stops that user authenticating at their next login, with no separate VPN user list to prune. Adding SCIM provisioning from the identity provider makes it explicit rather than passive: a deprovisioning event removes the user and their peers instead of waiting for a failed login. SCIM is an Enterprise feature.

The dependency worth surfacing now: SCIM needs an identity provider that can send it, and ADFS does not do so natively. If a tight offboarding SLA is a hard requirement, that pushes the Phase 1 identity decision toward Keycloak. This is one decision, not two, which is exactly why it is better decided than discovered.

Backing up the configuration In scope

The equivalent of your scheduled FortiGate config backup is a scheduled dump of the management datastore to the same backup server, on the same retention. This is the main reason for recommending PostgreSQL over the embedded store in Phase 1: it can be dumped live without stopping the service, and the external datastore is also what later enables an active-active control plane. Back up the database plus the management configuration and certificates.

For the restore runbook, note that peer private keys are not held in the datastore. A control-plane restore re-establishes identity, policy and routing, and peers re-key on reconnect. That is correct behaviour, and worth knowing so a restore test is not misread as a failure.

Connectivity for CI runners In scope

The runners become peers and receive a policy granting exactly the development resources they need, the same model as users but for machines. The part that removes the toil is ephemeral peers: a runner spun up per job enrols with an ephemeral setup key and NetBird removes the peer automatically once it goes offline, so no dead peers accumulate and there is no standing tunnel or bastion to maintain. Long-lived runners use an ordinary setup key. The control that matters here is key scope and rotation, since these are machine identities.

Cloudflare origin IP allowlisting Mostly out of scope

NetBird does not track Cloudflare's published ranges and will not maintain the FortiGate allowlist. What it can do is change the shape of the problem: where those services are reached today only over a public, Cloudflare-fronted path, the overlay can provide a private path instead, which removes the reason for some of those entries rather than automating them. For services you do not control, it does nothing.

If the actual pain is simply maintaining a list that changes, the honest fix is independent of NetBird: a scheduled job that pulls the published ranges and updates a FortiGate address object through the API. I can scope that separately, but I do not want to present it as a NetBird capability.

Assumptions
  • The identity provider chosen in Phase 1 supports SCIM, or the offboarding SLA can be met by login expiration plus scripted removal.
  • Runners run somewhere the agent can be installed, and ephemeral runners can execute a pre-job enrolment step.
To confirm
  • What offboarding SLA your policy requires, and whether contractors live in the same directory as employees.
  • Exactly what the runners need to reach, by host and port, so the policy is tight rather than broad.
  • Which Cloudflare-fronted services are involved, and whether the goal is to stop patching a list or to change how those services are reached.

05Phase 3 | Replacing site-to-site

The most consequential phase, and the one I would start only once remote access is proven in production. The FortiGates and their tunnels stay up throughout.

ReplacementHow the IPsec tunnels become routing peers

Each site gets a pair of routing peers joined to the same overlay as HQ, each advertising its local subnet as a resource: 10.60.0.0/16 at Site A, 10.70.0.0/16 at Site B, 10.80.0.0/16 at Site C, with HQ advertising 10.120.0.0/16. Once those routes exist and policy permits, a host at a site reaches HQ, or another site, by its normal address. It is the same routing-peer mechanism as Phase 1, applied site to site rather than user to HQ.

TopologyWhat the network looks like when complete

One overlay, with HQ and all three sites joined through routing-peer pairs and remote users on the same fabric. The important property is that peers build direct encrypted tunnels to each other, so traffic between Site A and Site B is not backhauled through HQ the way the current hub-and-spoke IPsec arrangement requires. The management, signal and relay services coordinate connections and can relay when NAT blocks a direct path, but they are not a required hop in the data path.

The FortiGates remain at each location as local firewalls and segmentation enforcement. What they stop being is the WAN.

High availabilityMatching the redundant FortiGate pairs

The redundant pair becomes a redundant routing-peer pair. NetBird supports high-availability routes, where multiple peers serve the same network and traffic fails over between them by metric, so if the primary peer at a site becomes unavailable the secondary carries the subnet. Active-active redundancy of the control plane itself is a separate concern and an Enterprise feature; for the data path, the redundancy that matters day to day is peer failover, which is in the self-hosted build.

Worth stating plainly: this is route failover, not the identical failure model as FortiGate HA, which may be doing sub-second stateful failover across a dedicated heartbeat. Overlay failover is fast but not the same thing, and the site pilot should measure it against what your sites actually require.

RisksLimitations and gotchas, including SCADA

SCADA stays off the overlay. No routing peer advertises those segments and no policy grants a path to them, so the segmentation you have today is unchanged. This is where the Networks model helps for evidence purposes: reachability is explicit, so "the new remote-access and WAN platform has no route to the SCADA segment" is a statement you can demonstrate rather than assert.

Risks I would put on the table
  • SCADA adjacency. The routing peers sit on the site network near that segmentation boundary. I would review their placement and the site firewall rules with whoever owns that boundary, so a peer can never become an inadvertent bridge, and document that review as evidence.
  • Internet dependency. IPsec today may run over circuits with their own SLAs. The overlay runs over each site's internet connection, so remote access and site connectivity may end up sharing a failure domain. Worth checking per site.
  • Blast radius. Consolidating both onto one platform is simpler to operate but makes the control plane WAN-critical rather than remote-access-critical. That raises the value of the backup work in Phase 2 and of an active-active control plane.
  • Cryptographic posture. If your current site-to-site IPsec runs in a FIPS-validated mode and those links carry CUI, moving them to WireGuard is a real change. See section 6.
Assumptions
  • Each site can host one or two Linux hosts as routing peers, with a route to the local subnet and outbound internet access.
  • Site subnets do not overlap, which they do not today.
To confirm
  • What failover behaviour the sites actually require, and whether inter-site traffic carries CUI.
  • The per-site internet links and their SLAs, and whether IPsec runs over dedicated circuits today.
  • Which site would be least disruptive to pilot, so the highest-risk site goes last rather than first.

06Design decisions

Three calls that shape the design rather than the build. Two are recommendations; one needs validating with your assessor.

Identity: keep AD and Duo, federate to them

Rather than moving identity, put a federation layer in front of what you already run, so MFA stays with Duo and authorization stays driven by AD group membership. ADFS is the shortest path where Windows licensing exists, Keycloak the strongest alternative and the better answer if SCIM offboarding matters, and Duo SSO by far the simplest if a hosted identity provider is acceptable given Duo is already a cloud dependency today.

Attribution: keep masquerade, take audit from the platform

Keeping masquerade is what lets Phase 1 deploy without touching a single firewall rule, and the per-user attribution requirement is then met by NetBird's traffic events rather than by firewall logs. The alternative, disabling masquerade to preserve source IPs, means adding overlay return routes across your internal networks, is supported on Linux routing peers only, and makes peer failover materially more complex because the return path has to follow the active peer. That is a lot of network change to satisfy a requirement the access platform can satisfy directly.

FIPS-validated cryptography (3.13.11)

WireGuard secures every tunnel with modern cryptography: ChaCha20-Poly1305, Curve25519 and BLAKE2s. That is not a FIPS-approved construction, and whether 3.13.11 binds depends on scoping I cannot do for you: whether the tunnel is the mechanism protecting CUI confidentiality or a transport over paths already protected by other means, and how your assessor has treated this previously.

My recommendation is to validate this early, with your compliance lead, before Phase 3 in particular. If it turns out to bind on specific paths, that does not undo this design: IPsec can remain on any path that must stay FIPS-validated while everything else moves. Knowing that now is worth more than discovering it during an assessment.

Enterprise features this design uses

Per-user traffic logging, SCIM provisioning, and an active-active control plane are NetBird Enterprise features. The open-source build you already tested runs everything else in this design.

07Rollout

Parallel throughout. At every stage the path being replaced is still available.

StageWhat happensFallback
1 | PilotFive to eight users, chosen for range of use cases, roughly two weeks. Validate NAT traversal, measure how much traffic relays, confirm DNS, find the applications nobody rememberedSSL VPN

One sequencing note: do not decommission SSL VPN as the last act of Phase 1 if Phase 3 is going ahead. The FortiGates remain your site-to-site infrastructure until Phase 3 completes, and keeping a known-good remote access path available while the WAN is reworked is cheap insurance.
